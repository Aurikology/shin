# Every way to get product prices, sweep of 2026-09-27

Jamin's ask, 2026-09-27: "search the web and look for every possible way we can get access to
product price information". Six Sonnet workers (WebSearch and WebFetch only) each took one class
of method; everything in `docs/lookup-alternatives-2026-09-23.md` was handed to them as already
covered. Their six files are kept verbatim below. This header is the reviewing session's.

## Limits, read first

- **The sweep is incomplete, and says where.** The 200-call web-search budget is shared by the
  whole session; all six workers hit it mid-lane and finished on direct page reads. Each file
  ends with a "Not opened" list. Lane 2 alone left 14 named vendors unopened. A missing source
  here is unsearched, not absent.
- **Workers' row counts were not trusted.** Lane 4 reported "~90 rows"; its tables hold 53.
- **No source here has been tested on a Canadian barcode by Shin.** Checks below are one fetch each.

## Every method found, by class (merged across the 2026-09-23 doc and this sweep)

1. **Official files the government or a crown retailer publishes:** BC Liquor price list (UPC,
   current), Beer Store partner list (UPC, 2024), LCBO/SAQ/OCS feeds (lane 6), StatCan average
   prices (category averages, no barcode), Ontario drug formulary prices.
2. **Laws abroad forcing stores to publish price files** (Israel, Croatia, Serbia, Hungary,
   Greece, Romania, Mexico PROFECO): not Canadian coverage, a model for what to ask for.
3. **Affiliate / publisher product feeds:** FlexOffers (UPC confirmed in feed spec), Rakuten
   (GTIN in spec; Well.ca, Sephora), AWIN (GTIN unvalidated), CJ, Impact. Free to join, needs
   approval per retailer.
4. **Google Merchant Center price benchmarks:** free, GTIN-matched, per country, but only for
   barcodes Shin lists as a merchant. Untested whether that is allowed for a non-seller.
5. **Store websites read directly:** PC Express (Loblaw banners), Pattison mi9cloud (Save-On,
   PriceSmart, Urban Fare, Fresh St.), T&T GraphQL, Shopify `/products.json` stores, Instacart
   storefronts (Rexall). Legal exposure in Canada: *Century 21 v Rogers* (2011 BCSC 1196)
   enforced browse-wrap terms against a bot.
6. **Flyers:** Flipp's endpoint (two live Canadian GitHub trackers use it), Actowiz sells a Flipp
   Canada dataset. Sale prices only.
7. **Paid price data:** MealMe, Skuuudle (Save-On-Foods a customer), DataWeave, Numerator Canada,
   Prisync, eezly, Savvi, Bright Data datasets. Quote-only.
8. **Receipts and shoppers:** Veryfi e-receipt ingestion (claims Loblaw/Walmart/Costco/Metro,
   $0.08-0.16), Caddle, shelf-tag photos, GasBuddy-style "still this price?".
9. **Archives:** Web Data Commons schema.org Offer extraction from Common Crawl (price and
   gtin13 where a site marks it up), Wayback Machine.
10. **Category APIs:** PriceCharting (games, UPC), IsThereAnyDeal (games, CAD), Discogs, eBay.


## Re-ranked by what users will actually scan (added 2026-09-27, his follow-up)

His words: "consider what users are actrually going to be using shin for". The list above was
ranked by how clean a source is (official, barcode-keyed, free), which put liquor boards first.
That is the wrong axis. Re-ranked here by the moment a user is standing in.

**What is known about real use, counted:** nothing yet. The PC's scan log holds 26 scans, every
one a team test of groceries (Lay's, Kraft Dinner, Green Giant, "unicorn steak"); the Mac's is
the same size class (`docs/answer-change-2026-09-26.md`: 3 distinct scanned barcodes). The mix
below comes from his rulings and the competitor study, not from users. The first tester week's
scan log settles it per kind.

**What the evidence says users will do:**
- His scope: "scan anything", "furniture, tech and much more", "its a search and find the price of
  anything app", global from the start (RULINGS.md, Catalogue scope). The one-sentence promise
  is "buy it, walk, or here is the cheaper one and where" (`docs/the-vision.md`).
- The apps that already do "point the phone, get a verdict" are almost all thrift and resale
  scanners pricing from sold listings (`research/competitors/scan-and-verdict-apps.md`: ThriftAI
  4.8 with 20K ratings, OLMA, Underpriced, Price Snap, ReSell AI, ResaleScan). The 09-03 memo
  made second-hand electronics the first "Ship" class; its 60/15/25 used/new/grocery mix is an
  assumption it labels unmeasured.

**Moment 1: buying something used** (thrift store, garage sale, Marketplace or Kijiji listing).
Usually no barcode; the photo names the model. The fair price is what that model sells for used.
- eBay Browse API (official, free, ebay.ca listings, asking prices): the one legitimate live
  source. Already known; not wired.
- eBay Marketplace Insights (sold prices, takes GTIN, needs a company to apply): the sold-price
  source this moment needs most. eBay closed signed-out sold data in Jul/Aug 2026 (09-03 memo).
- Kijiji asking prices (Apify scrapers, about $0.95 per 1,000 results as cited), StockX/GOAT for
  sneakers, PriceCharting for games and cards (takes UPC).
- Books (ISBN) and records (Discogs) are thrift-store staples; parked for the MVP on 09-26 until
  the miss log records one. Watch that log for them first.
- Constraint: RULINGS.md keeps marketplace/US listings off the price line, so used prices need
  their own ruling on how they are shown.

**Moment 2: a bigger purchase, new, in a store** (TV, headphones, appliance, tool, furniture,
baby gear, skincare). A barcode or a model number on the tag; the question is "is it cheaper
elsewhere or was it lower last month".
- Google Shopping through DataForSEO: already the live call.
- Affiliate feeds: Canadian Tire and Home Depot Canada on Impact, Staples on CJ, Well.ca and
  Sephora on Rakuten, FlexOffers (UPC in its feeds). Free to join, per-store approval.
- Keepa for Amazon.ca price history (already known), PCPartPicker for computer parts,
  Canadian Tire's internal API (open-source tracker exists).
- Google Merchant Center benchmark price per GTIN, if listing as a merchant is allowed.

**Moment 3: groceries and household, weekly shop.** Cheap per item, many items. The useful answer
is "this is on sale elsewhere this week" or "this is above its usual price", so flyer sale prices
matter more here than the header's "sale prices only" dismissal implied.
- Flipp's flyer data (two live Canadian trackers use it), PC Express (Loblaw banners), the
  Pattison backend (Save-On-Foods and three sister chains), Shin's own shelf-tag and typed prices,
  Veryfi-read receipts.

**Pushed to the bottom as not what users scan:** BC Liquor and Beer Store lists, StatCan averages,
the foreign price-transparency laws, IsThereAnyDeal, Wine-Searcher, fuel, cannabis.

**What would make this ranking wrong:** testers scanning mostly groceries after all, as every
team test so far has. The first week of tester scans, split by kind, is the check.


## Measured 2026-09-28: Save-On-Foods answers a barcode directly

His question: "What is the most reasonable thing we can do right now to get prices on the items in
our catalogue" (used goods ruled out the same message).

- `GET https://storefrontgateway.saveonfoods.com/api/stores/{retailerStoreId}/products/{GTIN-14}`
  returns JSON with `price`, `wasPrice`, `unitPrice`, `promotions`, `available`, keyed by the
  barcode itself, so the join is exact, never a name match. 404 when the store does not carry it.
- Answers a plain request with an honest user agent (`ShinPriceCheck/0.1`, and `curl/8.9`); no
  header spoofing, no key, no login. The site's search, sitemap and robots.txt sit behind a
  Cloudflare challenge; this endpoint does not. Legal note, marked not blocking: Save-On's terms
  were not read, and *Century 21 v Rogers* (2011 BCSC 1196) enforced browse-wrap terms against a bot.
- 196 stores in the store list (Save-On, PriceSmart, Urban Fare, Fresh St.; Western Canada).
- **Hit rate, counted, store 1982 (Langley):** 13 of 300 random Canadian Open Food Facts barcodes
  priced (4.3%); 20 of 60 barcodes Canadians recorded buying in Open Prices priced (33%).
- Derived, not measured: 4.3% of 121,949 Canadian grocery rows is about 5,300 priced products,
  against 862 catalogue products priced today. At 1.2 s a request one full pass is about 41 hours.
- A name search (`/preview?q=`) also answers but returns at most 3 products, so barcode lookup
  is the route.

## Top picks for Shin

1. **Serbia's September 2026 price-list regulation** is the only law found anywhere in this sweep that explicitly requires **barcode** as a published field alongside retail price and unit price, machine-readable, per store. It proves a "forced, barcode-keyed, machine-readable retail price file" regime is a real regulatory template, worth a one-page brief to whoever tracks Canadian policy asks, and worth trying to actually pull one live Serbian retailer's published file to see the real schema.
2. **rrrrvvvv/grocery-comparator and nupoor1/Grocer** were both pushed to GitHub in the last three days and both go through Flipp's public flyer-search endpoint rather than any single retailer's private API, covering most major Canadian chains. This is a live, working, free method right now, prove it by cloning either repo and diffing 50 SKUs against Shin's own 13,537-item crawl.
3. **Mexico's PROFECO "Quién es Quién en los Precios"** is the largest free, government-run, multi-retailer, multi-year (2015-present) retail price open dataset found in this entire sweep, with documented bulk files and an API, but its own data dictionary has no barcode/GTIN field, only product name + brand + store. Prove it by downloading one month's file and checking whether product-name + brand fuzzy-matching against Shin's own catalog gets usable barcode-level hits.
4. **Hungary's árfigyelő (arfigyelo.gvh.hu)** is the clearest working example of "retailers legally required to upload daily prices" for six major chains, ~2,000 products, the strongest real-world proof that mandatory daily retailer price feeds work at scale. Its own bulk-download/API mechanics were not checked this sweep; that's the next thing to prove.
5. **Web Data Commons' schema.org Product/Offer extraction from Common Crawl** (26M+ product offers, free, annual) is a zero-cost way to find Canadian retailer pages that already self-publish machine-readable price+GTIN13 in their own HTML. Prove it by grepping one year's release for `.ca` retailer domains and counting how many carry a `gtin13` field.

## Canadian legal position on scraping retailer prices

**Bottom line for Shin's use case (reading publicly displayed shelf prices, not personal data, not copying photos/descriptions wholesale): the strongest, most directly-on-point Canadian authority is favorable to a narrowly-scoped price-only scrape, but the contract/ToS route is where real Canadian retailers have actually won.**

### Copyright Act, facts are not protected
- **CCH Canadian Ltd. v. Law Society of Upper Canada**, 2004 SCC 13: the Supreme Court of Canada set the Canadian originality standard as "skill and judgment," rejecting both the low US "sweat of the brow" standard and a high creativity standard. Directly relevant holding as read: **raw facts and mechanical compilations are not copyrightable**; only work product involving skill and judgment (like the legal headnotes at issue in that case) is protected. A bare price number attached to a bare product identifier is a fact, not a copyrightable expression, this is the strongest textual support for "scraping the price itself is not a copyright act violation." (Confirmed via Wikipedia summary of the SCC decision; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCH_Canadian_Ltd_v_Law_Society_of_Upper_Canada, a primary-source CanLII read of the decision itself was not done this session.)
- **Tele-Direct (Publications) Inc. v. American Business Information, Inc.** (Federal Court of Appeal, 1997) is the leading Canadian case for the proposition that a database/compilation of facts (there, a business directory) needs originality in the *selection or arrangement* of the facts to be protected, the underlying facts (names, addresses, phone numbers, analogous to prices) remain free to copy. **This session did not independently re-verify Tele-Direct's holding by URL (search budget exhausted before reaching it), treat as unconfirmed-this-session, consistent with prior general knowledge, and re-verify before it goes in anything relied upon.**

### Contract / browse-wrap, this is where retailers have actually won in Canada
- **Century 21 Canada Limited Partnership v. Rogers Communications Inc., 2011 BCSC 1196**: Zoocasa (Rogers) ran a bot that scraped and re-published Century 21's real-estate listing photos and descriptions. The BC Supreme Court held Zoocasa liable for **both copyright infringement (photos/descriptions, not facts) and breach of contract**, the site's browse-wrap Terms of Use, which purported to forbid scraping/bots, was found **enforceable even though the bot never "clicked through" or saw the terms in the way a human would**. Remedy: injunction plus nominal damages ($1,000) to Century 21 (no real loss shown) but $32,000 to the individual brokers whose listings were used. **Key risk for Shin: if a retailer's ToS explicitly prohibits automated access/scraping, a Canadian court has already enforced that prohibition via contract law even against a non-clicking bot.** Source: https://canliiconnects.org/en/summaries/34793 ; https://www.slaw.ca/2011/10/21/browsewrap-contract-upheld-in-canada/
- **Trader Corporation v. CarGurus Inc., 2017 ONSC 1841**: CarGurus scraped ~152,532 vehicle photographs (not prices/facts) from dealer websites that Trader (AutoTrader.ca) had taken and licensed. Ontario Superior Court awarded **$305,064 in statutory damages** and, significantly, was **the first Canadian case to reject the "information location tool" (search-engine) safe harbour and reject a fair-dealing defence** for a commercial data aggregator scraping a competitor's content wholesale. **This case is about photographs (creative works), not bare facts/prices, it does not directly touch whether scraping a price number is infringing, but it is the clearest Canadian precedent that "we're just an information location tool like a search engine" is not a reliable defence for a commercial scraper competing directly with the source.** Source: https://www.canlii.org/en/on/onsc/doc/2017/2017onsc1841/2017onsc1841.html ; https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/information-location-tool-and-fair-dealing-copyright-defenses-rejected-trader-v-cargurus

### Criminal Code s.342.1, "unauthorized use of computer"
As read directly from the statute (laws-lois.justice.gc.ca): the offence requires acting **"fraudulently and without colour of right"** to (1) obtain computer services, (2) intercept computer functions via a device, (3) use a computer system with intent to commit those offences or mischief, or (4) traffic in a password enabling the above. Indictable, up to 10 years. **Reading publicly displayed prices from a public product page, with no login bypass, no password circumvention, and no intent to defraud, sits outside the plain elements of this section as read**, but a scraper that circumvents rate-limiting/IP-blocking specifically designed to keep it out (e.g. spoofing headers to defeat Akamai bot detection, as several of the reverse-engineered grocery APIs above openly do) moves closer to the "without colour of right" line and has not been tested against s.342.1 in a reported Canadian case that this session found. Source: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-342.1.html

### Trespass to chattels, a US doctrine, not established in Canadian law
Read directly: trespass to chattels was used against scraping in the US in **eBay v. Bidder's Edge** (2000) (bots draining even a small share of server resources was enough) and **Register.com v. Verio** (2000), then narrowed by **Intel Corp. v. Hamidi** (2003), which required actual interference with computer functioning, not just unwanted access. **This doctrine is explicitly US-centered; the Wikipedia source read confirms Canada is a common-law jurisdiction that recognizes trespass to chattels in principle but supplies no Canadian case law applying it to computer/scraping intrusion.** Treat as **not established** in Canada for this fact pattern, a genuine gap, not a "no." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trespass_to_chattels

### PIPEDA, relevance is narrow for Shin's use case
Shin is scraping **product prices**, not personal information, so PIPEDA's core "personal information" trigger likely does not apply to the price data itself. However, **if a scrape incidentally captures personal data** (e.g. a review with a name, a store employee's name on a page) PIPEDA's consent/purpose-limitation rules would apply to that portion. There is a real 2023 joint statement by the Canadian Privacy Commissioner's office (with international counterparts) on data scraping and privacy, **this session could not confirm its exact URL or quoted text** (both direct-URL guesses to priv.gc.ca 404'd and the web-search budget ran out before a working link could be found); flag this as **unconfirmed this session**, not fabricated, and re-verify before citing its specific language to Shin's counsel.

### Competition Act, no scraping-specific relevance found this session
No enforcement action or provision specifically addressing price-scraping was found or independently searched this session (budget exhausted before this query could be run). **Not opened**, flag as a gap, not a "no relevance."

### US persuasive authority (useful analogy, not binding in Canada)
- **hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn** (9th Cir., reaffirmed 2022): scraping **publicly accessible** web pages (no login required) does not violate the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act's "without authorization" prong, because a page with no access controls has no "authorization" to bypass. Important caveat also read: the case later **settled with hiQ paying damages after it was found to have used fake accounts to access non-public data**, the favorable CFAA ruling covered only the genuinely public portion. Source: https://www.eff.org/cases/hiq-v-linkedin ; https://calawyers.org/privacy-law/ninth-circuit-holds-data-scraping-is-legal-in-hiq-v-linkedin/
- **Meta Platforms, Inc. v. Bright Data Ltd.** (N.D. Cal., ruling read as of January 2024): a federal judge denied Meta summary judgment and found Bright Data's scraping of **publicly accessible** Facebook/Instagram data did **not** breach Meta's Terms of Use, rejecting Meta's contract-based theory for public data specifically. Directly useful analogy: **US courts are trending toward "public + no login + no bypassed technical barrier = not a contract breach," which is the opposite conclusion from Canada's own Century 21 v. Rogers, where a browse-wrap ban on bots WAS enforced.** This is the sharpest US/Canada divergence found this session and the single most important legal fact for Shin: **do not assume the US "public data scraping is fine" trend applies in Canada, Century 21 v. Rogers is Canadian, on point, and went the other way.** Source: fetched via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bright_Data (summary of the January 2024 ruling).

### Practical synthesis for Shin
1. Reading a bare price number off a public page is very likely not a **copyright** problem in Canada (CCH Canadian: facts/skill-judgment test; a price is a fact).
2. It **can** still be a **breach of contract** problem if the retailer's Terms of Use expressly forbid automated access/bots and Shin's method is found to access that site (Century 21 v. Rogers enforced exactly this, including against a non-consenting bot, via browse-wrap).
3. Circumventing an active bot-defense (Akamai, IP-blocking, header spoofing, used by several of the reverse-engineered APIs listed above) is not itself proven illegal under Criminal Code s.342.1 on a plain reading, but has not been tested in a reported Canadian case, so it is a **real, currently untested risk**, not a cleared one.
4. Prefer routes where the retailer or a licensed intermediary (Instacart, Flipp/FlyerKit, a retailer's own official developer API where one exists) is the effective publisher, since that shifts Shin from "scraping a site against its ToS" to "reading data the retailer chose to syndicate."
