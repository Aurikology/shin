The test used for "drastically hard"

A feature is drastically hard if it hits two or more of these, or one of them where the obstacle is an open problem rather than a task. Unfamiliar is not the same as hard, so nothing qualifies on my discomfort alone.

A
Data nobody sells you. No feed at any price, or no comparison set in existence.
B
An open matching problem. Recognising or comparing things that share no identifier.
C
Scale you do not have yet. Needs users, an audience, or reported volume before it works at all.
D
A cost that recurs per watched item forever, rather than per user or per build.
E
Legal or liability exposure that shapes the feature, not just the wording around it.

The hard ones are not twelve separate problems

They collapse into four, which is the useful part of this whole exercise.

Six of the twelve are one cost shape. Watch a price, tell me when a better source appears, a feed of drops, back in season, Shin was right, and the daily pick all require re-pricing things nobody is looking at. That cost scales with saved items multiplied by retailers multiplied by check frequency, and it never stops. It is also the retention mechanism and the monetising path, so the spend lands before the revenue does.

Three are the same missing asset: a trusted price for a specific store at a specific time, contributed by people. Corrections that train the next scan, a corrected price reused for that store, and reporting a fake all need it, and no vendor sells it. That is the moat and the reason it keeps reappearing.

Two are not engineering at all. The savings figure and selling pricing intelligence are a legal test and a different company. Building them is easy and neither is blocked by code.

One is a permanent tax disguised as a settings toggle, and it is the most underestimated row in the entire walkthrough. See number twelve.

The arithmetic behind the cost shape, since six features rest on it

1,000 users × 5 saved items × 2 checks a day × 3 retailers
= 30,000 lookups a day = 900,000 a month
at the grocery feed's published $0.75 per 1,000 rows
= $675 a month, at a thousand users, before one dollar of revenue

The $0.75 per thousand rows is the feed's published price. The five saves, two checks and three retailers are assumptions stated here so they can be argued with, and the $675 is arithmetic from them, not a measurement. Two consequences that matter more than the number. It scales with saves rather than users, so one power user with fifty watched items costs what ten casual users do, and your best users are your most expensive. And the throughput ceiling, not the monthly total, sets the plan you need: the Amazon price API's entry tier is twenty tokens a minute and its tokens expire after sixty minutes, so quota cannot be banked into a nightly sweep. Whether one token buys one lookup is not established, which makes the ceiling unknown rather than known.

The twelve, worked out

Each one: what the hard part actually is, what already exists to build on, the cheapest version that tests the same question, and what would prove the whole thing not worth doing.

01

Live camera recognition of arbitrary products

First-scan paths
Hard part
Criterion B, and it is the one obstacle on this list that money cannot move. Zero-shot fine-grained retail recognition measured 77.0% top-1 and 94.5% top-5 across 190 models on 409 products. One in four first guesses is wrong, and the walkthrough already cut this from the first version for exactly that reason.
What exists
The top-5 number is the design instruction: never show one guess, show a correctable name. Barcode scanning is a solved native capability and covers most packaged goods outright.
Cheap version
Barcode first, and when no barcode is in frame, offer three thumbnails instead of a verdict. Ships in days and tests the same question, which is whether people tolerate being asked.
Falsifier
If more than one scan in five needs correcting on real shelves, the camera is not an input method for this app, it is a demo for the videos.
02

Corrections that train the next scan

Identity options
Hard part
Criteria B and C together. A user fixing a name is one label. Turning labels into a better recogniser needs volume, a rule for conflicting corrections, and resistance to someone poisoning it deliberately. This is a live learning system, not a form.
What exists
The correction interface is trivial and the walkthrough already specifies it. Storing labelled pairs costs nothing. The hard half is only the part that closes the loop.
Cheap version
Collect the pairs, apply nothing. Correcting the index by hand once a week for the first few thousand corrections is faster and safer than any pipeline, and it tells you whether the corrections are even any good.
Falsifier
If a hand review of the first two hundred corrections finds most of them wrong, useless or adversarial, automating them would have made the product worse.
03

A corrected price reused for that store from then on

Disagreement paths
Hard part
Criteria A, C and D. This is the asset no competitor can buy, and that is exactly why it is hard. It needs a specific store rather than a chain, an expiry because a shelf price goes stale in days, a rule for two users reporting different numbers for the same shelf, and defence against a store employee or a rival reporting numbers that suit them.
What exists
A whole app is built on crowdsourced shelf prices, so the model is proven, not speculative. Receipt-scanning cashback apps hold the same data as a side effect and are the precedent for volume. Store identity can come from the phone's location at the moment of the scan.
Cheap version
Record every report with the store, the time and the user, show it back only to the person who reported it, and trust nothing. That is a week of work, and it produces the dataset that decides whether the trusted version is possible.
Falsifier
Fewer than one report per active user per week, or two independent reports for the same shelf disagreeing more often than they agree. Either kills the trusted layer, and the second one is the one to watch.
04

Watch a price, at a threshold Shin picks

Action options
Hard part
Criterion D, in its purest form, and it is the single most underestimated item on this list because the code is easy. Re-pricing everything nobody is looking at is a bill that arrives monthly and grows with your best users. The threshold also has to be defensible: suggesting a number means knowing the price distribution, not guessing a percentage.
What exists
This exact feature is the entire product for tools with 4.8 million users between them, which is proof of demand and a warning about the cost. Price history feeds sell the history you would need to set a threshold honestly.
Cheap version
Check watched items once a day, cap free watches at five, and set the threshold from the item's own recorded low rather than a percentage. Caps are not a monetising trick here, they are how the bill stays bounded while you learn the real usage.
Falsifier
If the ratio of distinct products to total scans stays near one, caching saves nothing and every watch is a fresh lookup. That single ratio decides whether this feature is affordable, and it is one of the three numbers to instrument on day one.
05

Tell me when it turns up cheaper somewhere else

Notification triggers
Hard part
Criterion D multiplied by every retailer you monitor. Watching one item at one store is one lookup; watching it everywhere is one lookup per retailer per check, forever, for an item the user may never buy.
What exists
One Canadian comparison service already tracks 32 retailers, which means the sweep exists as somebody else's product. Buying or partnering beats rebuilding it.
Cheap version
Recheck alternatives only when the user opens the item, not on a schedule. It is the same information at the moment they care, at a fraction of the cost, and it turns a background bill into a foreground one.
Falsifier
If a month of watched items produces almost no cross-retailer wins, the sweep is paying for a notification that rarely fires.
06

Alternatives that taste the same

Action options, and your original design
Hard part
Criteria B and E. "Cheaper in the same category" is easy and already solved by the unit prices in the grocery feeds. "Tastes the same" is a claim about a product you did not make, and a food swap suggestion puts you in product liability territory the moment allergens differ. The recommendation is easy; standing behind the sentence is not.
What exists
Yuka's alternatives run on same-category-better-score rather than any similarity model, which is a mechanism you can copy exactly. Open food data covers ingredients and allergens for packaged goods, which is what the copy has to respect.
Cheap version
Same category, cheaper per unit, and let Shin say "cheaper per 100 grams" instead of "tastes the same." Identical feature, no claim, and it is more useful because it is checkable.
Falsifier
If people tap alternatives and buy nothing, this is a feature that demos well in a video and does nothing in a store, which is worth knowing before the taste claim is ever written.
07

A feed of drops on things like what you scan

Home screen options
Hard part
Criteria A, C and D at once, which makes it the hardest single row in the walkthrough. A recommender needs a catalogue you do not own, similarity you do not have, and continuous pricing of a candidate pool far larger than what anyone scanned. The walkthrough's own warning applies too: an empty feed kills an app faster than no feed.
What exists
Nothing that shortens this. It is a later-stage system that assumes the other eleven work.
Cheap version
One item a day, chosen by hand, from the category the videos perform in. Editorial rather than algorithmic. It tests whether anyone opens the app for something they did not scan, which is the actual question hiding under the feed.
Falsifier
If the hand-picked daily item gets no taps, no recommender was going to rescue it.
08

Back in season

Notification triggers
Hard part
Criterion A, in the one category the pilot ruled out. It needs a seasonal baseline per item per region, which means at least a year of history before the first notification can fire, and it sits on produce, where a sticker code names a category rather than a product and prices moved 4.7% in a single month this July.
What exists
National monthly fresh fruit and vegetable series are published and free, which is enough to say "cucumbers are unusually expensive this month" at a country level. It is not enough to say anything about your store.
Cheap version
Use the public national series to caption a verdict, with the source shown: "fresh vegetables are up 5.5% this month nationally." Honest, sourced, no baseline of your own required.
Falsifier
Produce scans staying a small share of total scans. If nobody scans produce, this notification has no audience.
09

Monthly wrapped, what Shin saved you

Share formats
Hard part
Criterion E, plus a definition problem underneath it. Section 74.01(1)(b) of the Competition Act requires a performance claim be backed by adequate and proper testing done before the claim is made, and it covers in-app copy. Worse than the law: "saved you" assumes the user would otherwise have bought at the higher price, and you cannot observe that. A shareable number you cannot defend is the most dangerous asset in the product.
What exists
Everything you need for a defensible version, because every verdict already records both prices and the comparison set behind them.
Cheap version
Report what happened instead of what was saved. "You checked 47 things. Shin told you to walk away from 12." Both numbers are observed facts, both are shareable, and neither is a performance claim.
Falsifier
Nothing kills this feature; the claim version is simply not available until the number has been tested. It is a gate with a key, not a wall.
10

Selling aggregate pricing intelligence

Revenue options
Hard part
Criteria C and E, and it is a different company rather than a feature. It needs volume that makes the aggregate meaningful, the right to resell what users contributed, privacy compliance over stored location and shopping history, and an enterprise sales motion that shares nothing with a consumer app.
What exists
Commercial licensing of Canadian grocery price panels already happens, so the buyer exists and the price is discoverable. That is worth knowing and worth nothing until you have the volume.
Cheap version
Write the data rights into the terms of service on day one so the option stays open, and build none of it. That is an afternoon and it is the whole of what should be done now.
Falsifier
Not applicable until there is volume. Revisit when the reported-price layer has real coverage in one city.
11

Reading the price straight off the shelf tag

First-scan paths
Hard part
Criteria A and B, though of a tedious rather than deep kind. Reading characters is solved. Knowing which number on the tag is the price, which is the unit price, which is the club price and which is the expiry date is a layout problem that differs per retailer and gets redesigned without telling you.
What exists
On-device text recognition ships free in both mobile platforms. Tag layouts are consistent within a banner, so this is a finite set of templates rather than an open problem.
Cheap version
Read every number on the tag, show them, and let the user tap the price. That sidesteps the layout problem entirely and produces the labelled data that would later automate it.
Falsifier
If users abandon the tap-the-price step, tag reading is not a path anyone wants and the barcode is the only in-store input.
12

Pick Shin's attitude: polite, blunt, or unhinged

Introduction options
Hard part
None of the five criteria, which is exactly why it belongs here. It is one row in a table and a toggle in settings, and it triples every string in the product, permanently. Each verdict has its own sentence per category, and there are four categories, so this multiplies the copy surface that carries the entire personality of the app, forever, including everything you have not written yet.
What exists
Nothing makes this cheaper. Generating tone variants with a model at runtime trades the cost for an unpredictable voice, which for a mascot is the one thing not to risk.
Cheap version
One voice, chosen by whichever caption won the video test. The winning caption is already telling you the tone, so this decision is made by evidence you will have rather than a setting the user has to think about.
Falsifier
Users asking for a gentler Shin in reviews. That is the only signal worth reopening this for, and it costs nothing to wait for.

The one thing to say about order

Building the hard twelve first is right for the reason you are probably asking: hard is where the unknowns are, and unknowns are what a plan should attack early. But hard is not the same as irreversible, and the two irreversible things stay in front of it, because a name and a first video are spent once while a hard feature is rebuilt at leisure. Every entry above has a cheap version listed for a reason: each of those tests the same question the hard version answers, for days of work instead of months, and eleven of the twelve can be settled that way before anything expensive gets built.

Derived by arithmetic and labelled as such: the $675 monthly watch cost, from the feed price and three stated assumptions about saves, check frequency and retailer count.
